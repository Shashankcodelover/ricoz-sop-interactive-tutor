1.1 Purpose of the Playbook
The purpose of this playbook is to establish a standardized, accountable, measurable, and repeatable operating
framework for taking a product from initial ideation to market launch within a structured 30-day execution cycle.
This playbook is designed to coordinate five core departments—Product, Technology, Marketing, People, and
Operations—under a single integrated execution plan.
It defines the responsibilities, deliverables, dependencies, approval mechanisms, performance standards, and operational
procedures required to ensure that every product progresses through a consistent lifecycle.
The playbook is intended to eliminate ambiguity regarding ownership, prevent delays caused by cross-functional
dependencies, improve execution transparency, and establish clear accountability for achieving product and business
outcomes.
Primary Objective: Enable the organization to validate, develop, prepare, launch, and initially optimize a product
through a coordinated 30-day operating cycle, targeting Day 25 for Go-To-Market execution.
The 30-day target applies to products whose scope, complexity, regulatory requirements, and available resources make
such a launch feasible. Where these conditions are not met, the responsible leadership must formally approve a revised
launch scope or timeline rather than bypass mandatory quality or compliance requirements.
1.2 Strategic Objectives
The playbook is built around eight strategic objectives.
Objective 1 — Standardize Product Execution
Establish a uniform operating process across the organization's product portfolio.
Each product must follow the same fundamental structure for ideation, validation, planning, development, launch
preparation, execution, and review.
Product-specific requirements may vary, but the underlying governance and accountability framework must remain
consistent.
Expected Outcome: Reduced process inconsistency and improved predictability of product execution.
Objective 2 — Establish Individual Accountability
Ensure that every activity is assigned to an identifiable individual rather than being owned only by a department.

Each task must have a responsible owner, an accountable reviewer, a defined deadline, and measurable completion
criteria.
Expected Outcome: Clear responsibility for execution, review, and delivery.
Objective 3 — Enable Cross-Functional Coordination
Create an integrated operating structure in which all five departments execute their responsibilities in parallel while
managing dependencies systematically.
Departments must communicate required inputs, handoffs, blockers, and approvals through documented processes.
Expected Outcome: Fewer handoff delays and stronger alignment between product development and business
readiness.
Objective 4 — Accelerate Time-to-Market
Establish a disciplined 30-day operating cycle with a target GTM launch on Day 25.
Execution speed must be achieved through appropriate prioritization, parallel workstreams, early validation, reusable
systems, and effective resource allocation.
Expected Outcome: Faster movement from validated product opportunity to initial market entry without compromising
mandatory launch standards.
Objective 5 — Build Market-Ready Products
Ensure that products are not considered launch-ready solely because development has been completed.
Product readiness must include usability, reliability, positioning, customer communication, onboarding, support,
operational workflows, and commercial preparedness.
Expected Outcome: A product that can be delivered, understood, adopted, and supported by its intended customers.
Objective 6 — Introduce Evidence-Based Decision-Making
Require critical decisions to be supported by documented findings, testing results, performance data, and formal
approvals.
Assumptions must be distinguished from validated facts.
Expected Outcome: Better product prioritization, clearer risk management, and more defensible launch decisions.
Objective 7 — Establish Performance Visibility
Provide leadership with a consistent view of execution progress, department performance, unresolved dependencies,
launch readiness, and post-launch results.
Expected Outcome: Timely intervention and improved management control.
Objective 8 — Create a Repeatable Launch System
Ensure that lessons learned, successful execution methods, reusable assets, and operational improvements are
incorporated into future product launch cycles.
Expected Outcome: Continuous improvement in launch efficiency and execution quality across the product portfolio.

1.3 Scope and Applicability
This playbook applies to products and business initiatives that require coordinated execution across the five designated
departments.
Examples include:
● Software-as-a-Service products.
● Digital platforms and marketplaces.
● Web-based and mobile applications.
● Technology-enabled service products.
● New product launches and minimum viable products.
● Existing product relaunches and major feature introductions.
Each product must have its own execution instance, with unique task owners, deliverables, milestones, risks, and
performance targets.
The master playbook defines the standard operating method. The product-specific execution tracker records the actual
work performed.
Scope Boundaries
The 30-day execution cycle covers:
Pre-Launch: Ideation, discovery, validation, planning, design, development, testing, market preparation, team readiness,
and operational readiness.
Launch: Final approvals, production release, campaign activation, initial customer acquisition, and launch monitoring.
Post-Launch: Early customer onboarding, performance measurement, issue resolution, optimization, and preparation for
the next execution cycle.
Long-term product scaling, major platform expansion, and sustained growth operations are managed through subsequent
operating cycles.
1.4 Execution Principles
All departments must follow the principles below.
Principle 1 — One Task, One Responsible Owner
Every task must have exactly one directly responsible individual.
Multiple contributors may support the activity, but responsibility for completing and updating the task must remain
unambiguous.
Principle 2 — No Activity Without a Defined Deliverable
Every planned activity must produce a tangible output, documented decision, verified outcome, or measurable result.
Examples include an approved product brief, completed design file, tested feature, published landing page, or signed
operational checklist.
Principle 3 — No Completion Without Evidence

-- 4 of 211 --

A task cannot be marked as completed merely because the responsible person reports that work has been performed.
Completion must be supported by evidence appropriate to the activity, such as a document, design link, code reference,
test report, published asset, approval record, or verified metric.
Principle 4 — Dependencies Must Be Explicit
Each department must identify the inputs required from other teams before starting dependent work.
Dependencies must have a supplying owner, receiving owner, required delivery date, and acceptance condition.
Principle 5 — Critical Risks Must Be Escalated Early
Risks affecting launch scope, security, compliance, customer experience, budget, or the critical path must be reported
promptly.
Responsible individuals must not wait for the next weekly review when an issue threatens an approved milestone.
Principle 6 — Quality Gates Cannot Be Bypassed Informally
Formal launch checkpoints must be completed before the product advances to the next controlled stage.
An exception may be considered only where it does not violate mandatory security, legal, regulatory, or customer safety
requirements and has been documented and approved by the authorized decision-maker.
Principle 7 — Reuse Before Rebuilding
Teams should prioritize approved reusable components, design systems, technical infrastructure, marketing templates,
SOPs, and operational processes wherever appropriate.
Principle 8 — Launch Readiness Is a Shared Responsibility
Technology readiness alone does not establish launch readiness.
A product must satisfy the applicable product, technical, marketing, people, and operational requirements before its
public launch.

1.6 Responsibility and Accountability Model
The organization will use a RACI-based responsibility framework.
R — Responsible: Individual performing and completing the work.
A — Accountable: Individual ultimately answerable for the outcome and authorized to accept or approve it.
C — Consulted: Individuals whose expertise or input is required.
I — Informed: Individuals who must receive relevant updates or decisions.
Each task must have one accountable role and at least one responsible person. For operational clarity, one responsible
person should be designated as the primary task owner.
The responsible and accountable roles may be held by the same individual when appropriate, particularly in smaller
product teams.

1.10 Escalation and Issue Management
Every task owner is responsible for identifying and reporting risks that may affect execution.
Escalation Levels
Level 1 — Task-Level Issue
Examples: Missing information, minor dependency delay, or routine execution clarification.
First Contact: Department Lead or supplying task owner.
Expected Response: Within four working hours for a time-sensitive issue.
Level 2 — Departmental or Cross-Functional Blocker
Examples: Resource conflict, missed critical dependency, unresolved requirement, or workstream delay.
Escalation Authority: Product Launch Owner and relevant Department Leads.
Expected Response: Within two working hours of escalation during the agreed working window.
Level 3 — Critical Launch Risk
Examples: Major security vulnerability, severe production instability, material compliance concern, unavailable essential
launch capability, or significant budget/scope conflict.
Escalation Authority: Executive Sponsor, Product Launch Owner, and responsible functional lead.
Expected Response: Immediate notification upon identification; an initial containment or decision plan should be
established as quickly as practicable.
Mandatory Issue Record
Each escalated issue must record:
● Issue ID and description.
● Date and time identified.
● Severity and business impact.
● Affected tasks and milestones.
● Responsible resolution owner.
● Proposed mitigation.
● Required decision.
● Target resolution time.
● Current status.
● Closure evidence.

1.11 Launch Readiness and Go/No-Go Governance
The Day 25 GTM launch is conditional upon documented readiness.
Mandatory Readiness Dimensions
Product Readiness: Approved launch scope, completed critical user journeys, accepted functionality, and documented
known limitations.
Technical Readiness: Successful deployment checks, acceptable reliability and performance, security validation,
monitoring, backup or recovery provisions where applicable, and no unresolved launch-blocking defects.
Marketing Readiness: Approved positioning, accurate customer-facing claims, completed launch assets, functioning
conversion paths, and campaign tracking.
People Readiness: Assigned launch coverage, confirmed availability of critical personnel, required training, and
documented escalation contacts.
Operational Readiness: Tested customer onboarding, support processes, payment or fulfillment workflows where
applicable, approved commercial terms, and required compliance checks.
Go/No-Go Decision Rules
GO: All mandatory readiness conditions have been met and required approvals have been recorded.
CONDITIONAL GO: Only noncritical exceptions remain; each exception has an approved mitigation, owner, deadline,
and documented acceptance of residual risk.
NO-GO: One or more mandatory conditions remain unmet, or a critical unresolved risk makes launch unacceptable.
A conditional approval cannot override legal, regulatory, security, or customer safety obligations.
The final decision must be recorded by the designated launch approval authority.

1.14 Definition of Success
A successful 30-day execution cycle is not defined solely by launching a product.
Success requires evidence that the organization has:
1. Validated a meaningful customer problem and product opportunity.
2. Approved and delivered a feasible launch scope.
3. Established reliable and usable product functionality.
4. Prepared the marketing and acquisition infrastructure.
5. Assigned and enabled the required people.
6. Established functional customer-facing operations.
7. Completed required quality and readiness reviews.
8. Executed an authorized GTM launch.
9. Measured initial customer and business outcomes.
10. Identified and assigned priorities for the next operating cycle.
Where launch is deferred for valid readiness reasons, the cycle must still conclude with a documented decision,
corrective action plan, and revised execution forecast.

2.1 Phase Purpose
Phase 1 establishes whether the proposed product represents a sufficiently meaningful, feasible and commercially
defensible opportunity to justify entering the structured execution cycle.
The objective is not simply to approve an idea. The objective is to determine whether the organization understands the
customer problem, can identify an appropriate initial market, possesses or can obtain the required delivery capabilities,
and can define a realistic launchable scope.
The five departments must conduct their assessments in parallel while sharing findings through a controlled execution
process.
By the end of Day 5, leadership should be able to answer:
1. What problem does the proposed product solve?
2. Who experiences this problem, and how frequently?
3. What evidence indicates that the problem is worth solving?
4. What solutions or alternatives currently exist?
5. Why might customers choose the proposed product?
6. What is the smallest credible version that can deliver value?
7. Is that version technically feasible within the available constraints?
8. What people, skills, budget and operational capabilities are required?
9. What assumptions remain unvalidated?
10. Should the organization proceed, pivot, defer or stop?
2.2 Phase-Wide Execution Rules
All task owners must follow these rules throughout Days 1–5.
Rule 1 — Record facts separately from assumptions. Research findings, interview observations, estimates and
hypotheses must be distinguishable.
Rule 2 — Avoid premature feature commitments. Product features identified during discovery remain candidates
until the approved MVP scope is established.
Rule 3 — Use shared evidence. All departments must reference the same current product brief, customer definition
and opportunity assumptions.
Rule 4 — Escalate launch-threatening findings immediately. Material technical, regulatory, financial or operational
concerns must not be deferred until Day 5.
Rule 5 — Record ownership and approval. Each primary task requires one named responsible individual and one
accountable reviewer.

Rule 6 — Apply proportional validation. Research depth should reflect product risk and complexity. A short discovery
cycle must not be presented as conclusive market validation.

Primary Responsible Role: Product Manager
Accountable Reviewer: Product Lead
Deadline: Day 1, before daily close
Priority: Critical
Task Objective
Establish a clear, consistent definition of the proposed product so that all departments evaluate the same opportunity.

Execution SOP
Step 1 — Collect the original idea.
Obtain the proposed product concept from the idea sponsor or originating stakeholder.
Record the idea's origin, intended customer, perceived problem, proposed solution and expected business value.
Do not assume that the proposed solution has already been validated.
Step 2 — Define the problem hypothesis.
Write a concise statement describing the problem the product intends to address.
Use the format:
"For [target customer], who experiences [specific problem], the proposed product aims to provide [intended outcome]
through [solution approach]."
Step 3 — Identify preliminary customer segments.
Identify the most likely primary customer segment and any important secondary segments.
Record relevant characteristics such as industry, user role, business size, geography, customer behavior or purchasing
context.
Step 4 — Describe the proposed value proposition.
Explain the intended benefit of the product, including time saved, cost reduced, revenue enabled, convenience improved
or another measurable outcome.
Step 5 — Record assumptions and unknowns.
Create an initial assumption list covering customer demand, technical feasibility, market differentiation, pricing,
acquisition and operational delivery.
Step 6 — Prepare the Product Opportunity Brief.
Create a structured document containing:
● Product working name and category.
● Idea originator and sponsor.
● Customer problem hypothesis.
● Intended customer segment.
● Proposed solution.
● Expected customer benefit.
● Preliminary business objective.
● Major assumptions.
● Known constraints.
● Questions requiring validation.
Step 7 — Conduct the initial alignment review.
Present the brief to the Product Launch Owner and department leads.
Capture corrections, disagreements and unanswered questions.
Required Deliverable
Product Opportunity Brief — Draft Version 0.1.

Step 4 — Identify high-risk technical requirements.
Assess potential concerns involving:
● Complex third-party integrations.
● Sensitive data processing.
● Security and privacy.
● High-volume transactions.
● Real-time processing.
● Payment systems.
● External platform dependencies.

2.9.3 Gate G1 Evaluation Scorecard
Use the following scoring model as an internal decision aid.
Dim ension 	Weight 	Evaluation focus
Customer Problem & Evidence 	25% 	Importance, frequency, severity, evidence quality
Market Opportunity 	20% 	Audience relevance, alternatives, differentiation
Technical Feasibility 	20% 	Implementation practicality and major dependencies
Commercial & Operational Feasibility 	15% 	Delivery model, costs, operational constraints
Resource Readiness 	10% 	Skills, availability and execution capacity
Strategic Alignment 	10% 	Fit w ith organizational priorities
Total 	100%
Each dimension receives a rating from 1 to 5:
1 — Weak: Significant concerns or little supporting evidence.
2 — Limited: Some supporting rationale but major gaps remain.
3 — Moderate: Plausible opportunity with identifiable uncertainties.
4 — Strong: Credible evidence and manageable constraints.
5 — Very Strong: Substantial supporting evidence and a clearly defensible execution case.
Weighted Score Formula:
Weighted Score (%) = Sum of (Dimension Rating ÷ 5 × Dimension Weight)
Suggested Interpretation
● 80–100%: Strong candidate for progression.
● 65–79%: Potential progression with documented conditions.
● Below 65%: Rework, additional validation, pivot or stop recommended.
These thresholds are suggested internal management defaults, not statistically validated indicators of product success.
A high aggregate score does not override critical technical, legal, regulatory, security, financial or operational concerns.

2.12 PHASE 01 — COMMON FAILURE MODES & CORRECTIVE ACTIONS
Failure 1 — The Idea Is Treated as Already Validated
Risk: Teams begin planning around unsupported customer assumptions.
Corrective Action: Require the Product Manager to separate evidence from assumptions and document the limitations
of discovery.
Failure 2 — MVP Scope Expands Too Early
Risk: Nonessential features make the Day 25 launch unrealistic.
Corrective Action: Prioritize the minimum end-to-end customer outcome and defer noncritical enhancements.
Failure 3 — Technical Risks Are Discovered Late
Risk: Essential integrations or architecture requirements invalidate the proposed schedule.
Corrective Action: Investigate the highest-risk technical assumptions during Days 1–4 and escalate blockers
immediately.
Failure 4 — Resource Availability Is Assumed
Risk: Work is assigned to individuals who lack capacity or required skills.
Corrective Action: Validate actual availability and obtain resource commitments before detailed execution planning.
Failure 5 — Marketing Begins Without a Clear Customer Definition
Risk: Positioning and acquisition efforts target an audience that is poorly aligned with the product.
Corrective Action: Base preliminary GTM planning on the agreed customer problem and audience evidence.

Failure 6 — Operational Delivery Is Considered After Development

Failure 7 — Approval Is Informal

Corrective Action: Record the Gate G1 decision, conditions, owner and approval authority in the controlled workspace.

2.14 PHASE PURPOSE & EXPECTED OUTCOMES
Phase 2 establishes the operational foundation required to execute the product's approved opportunity.
During Phase 1, the organization evaluated whether the proposed product should be pursued. During Phase 2, the
organization must determine precisely how the approved opportunity will be delivered.
The primary purpose is to eliminate uncertainty related to scope, requirements, ownership, execution sequence,
technical architecture, resources, market preparation and operational delivery before the intensive development cycle
begins.
The phase must produce a coordinated execution plan that is realistic, measurable and supported by documented
commitments from all five departments.
2.14.1 Primary Objectives
1. Translate the validated product opportunity into a structured Product Requirements Document (PRD).
2. Define and prioritize the Minimum Viable Product (MVP) scope.
3. Prepare the essential user journeys, wireframes and interface design specifications.
4. Finalize the technical architecture, development approach and integration requirements.
5. Establish the engineering backlog, delivery estimates and critical-path dependencies.
6. Define the product's brand positioning, messaging and GTM production requirements.
7. Confirm individual resource assignments, availability and capability readiness.
8. Design operational workflows, customer onboarding, support processes and commercial procedures.
9. Establish measurable departmental deliverables and acceptance standards.
10. Obtain formal approval to enter the build phase.
2.14.2 Phase Execution Principles
Scope discipline: Only approved MVP requirements may enter the committed development backlog.
Parallel execution: Departments should progress simultaneously wherever dependencies allow.
Evidence-based planning: Estimates, resource commitments and launch assumptions must be documented.
Single-point accountability: Every task must have one primary responsible individual and one accountable reviewer.
Design-to-development continuity: Product design outputs must be sufficiently detailed to support implementation.
Launch-oriented planning: All planning decisions must consider the Day 25 GTM target.
Controlled change management: Material changes after Gate G2 require impact assessment and approval.

Required Action: Assign a correction owner and target date based on risk.

Phase 4 verifies whether the integrated MVP can perform its intended functions under realistic conditions and whether
the supporting business organization is ready for controlled public launch.
The phase begins with the release candidate produced during Phase 3. All subsequent testing, fixes and configuration
changes must be traceable to an identified build version.
Unlike development, where the primary objective is implementing functionality, validation focuses on detecting failures,
verifying requirements, assessing customer experience, confirming operational reliability and producing credible
launch-readiness evidence.
2.40.1 Primary Objectives
1. Validate the approved MVP against functional and nonfunctional requirements.
2. Execute end-to-end functional, integration and regression testing.
3. Conduct user acceptance testing with representative users or authorized business representatives.

5. Verify the product's critical transaction, authentication and customer data flows.
6. Validate landing pages, conversion tracking, campaign attribution and customer acquisition workflows.
7. Test customer onboarding, support, commercial operations and exception handling.
8. Verify that launch-critical personnel understand their responsibilities.
9. Resolve or formally assess outstanding defects and readiness gaps.
10. Produce a consolidated readiness assessment for Gate G4.
2.40.2 Phase Execution Rules
Rule 1 — No untracked changes. Every code, configuration or customer-facing change must be recorded and
associated with the relevant release candidate.
Rule 2 — Evidence before closure. A test or defect cannot be marked passed or closed without documented
verification evidence.
Rule 3 — Independent validation. Wherever practical, the person verifying a critical feature should be different from
the person who implemented it.
Rule 4 — Risk-based coverage. Testing must prioritize core customer journeys, payments, identity, sensitive data,
external integrations and other high-impact functions relevant to the product.
Rule 5 — Production-like testing. Use a controlled environment that reasonably represents production configurations,
permissions, integrations and deployment behavior.

Rule 6 — Protect real customer information. Use synthetic or appropriately anonymized test data unless a separate
authorized process permits otherwise.
Rule 7 — No score-based safety override. Mandatory security, privacy, legal, financial and operational controls cannot
be bypassed through readiness scoring.
Rule 8 — Launch remains conditional. Gate G4 supports the Day 24 Go/No-Go decision but does not replace it.

2.57.3 Go/No-Go Decision Options
GO
All mandatory launch conditions are satisfied. The approved release may proceed during the designated launch window.
CONDITIONAL GO — PRE-EXPOSURE CONDITIONS
Only specifically identified, nonblocking administrative or operational conditions remain. Public exposure is prohibited
until the authorized verifier confirms their closure. This option must never be used to bypass mandatory safety, security,
legal or core-service requirements.
NO-GO
One or more mandatory launch conditions are not satisfied. Public release and related campaign activation are
prohibited.
DEFER
The launch is postponed because required work, approvals, capacity or external dependencies cannot be completed
responsibly within the planned window.
RESCOPE & REAUTHORIZE
A material scope change is required. The revised release must undergo appropriate reassessment and formal approval
before public exposure.

2.61.3 Immediate Pause or Rollback Triggers

2.67 PHASE PURPOSE, OPERATING CONTEXT & EXPECTED OUTCOMES
Phase 6 completes the 30-day product execution cycle by transitioning the product from controlled launch execution to
an evidence-based operating and improvement model.
The first five days following launch are particularly important because real customer activity may expose issues that were
not observed during development, QA or controlled acceptance testing.
The organization must distinguish between:
● Technical deployment success.
● Operational service reliability.
● Customer activation and satisfaction.
● Marketing acquisition performance.
● Commercial and financial viability.
● Organizational execution effectiveness.
These are related but separate outcomes. A product can be technically stable without meaningful customer adoption.
Similarly, initial registrations or campaign traffic do not establish product-market fit or long-term profitability.
The objective is therefore to build a reliable early performance baseline and make informed next-cycle decisions without
overstating what five days of market data can prove.
2.67.1 Primary Objectives
1. Maintain production reliability and customer service continuity.
2. Identify, classify and resolve launch-related defects and incidents.
3. Validate the product's real customer activation and usage patterns.
4. Identify friction in onboarding and critical customer journeys.
5. Evaluate early acquisition, conversion and marketing efficiency.
6. Monitor customer support, service delivery and commercial operations.
7. Assess staffing effectiveness, capability gaps and operational workload.
8. Review revenue, acquisition cost, operating cost and early unit economics where data is available.
9. Consolidate department-level KPIs and evidence.
10. Prioritize the next product, growth and operational improvement backlog.
11. Conduct Gate G7 and authorize the next operating cycle.
12. Establish accountable ownership beyond Day 30.

2.67.2 Phase Execution Principles
Principle 1 — Stabilization before expansion. Do not increase traffic, campaigns or feature scope while material
production risks remain unresolved.
Principle 2 — Customer impact drives priority. Production issues affecting customer safety, trust, data integrity,
payment or core service outcomes take precedence over optional enhancements.
Principle 3 — Controlled production changes. All fixes must undergo appropriate review, testing, deployment
authorization and monitoring.
Principle 4 — Evidence over assumptions. Performance conclusions must be based on observed data, with sample
size, measurement period and limitations disclosed.
Principle 5 — No artificial growth claims. Registrations, qualified leads, active users, revenue and retained
customers must be reported separately.
Principle 6 — Shared performance ownership. Product, Technology, Marketing, People and Operations each own
specific measures but remain accountable for cross-functional outcomes.
Principle 7 — Financial discipline. Campaign expenditure, infrastructure cost, operating cost and realized revenue
must be reconciled using consistent definitions.
Principle 8 — No premature product-market fit declaration. Five days of data may establish early directional
signals, not durable retention or proven long-term demand.
Principle 9 — Clear operational transition. Every continuing issue, process, metric and workstream must have a
named owner after Day 30.
Principle 10 — Decision-oriented closure. Gate G7 must produce an authorized decision, not merely a retrospective
presentation.

2.75.3 Financial Calculation Standards
Net Revenue = Gross Revenue − Refunds − Applicable Revenue Adjustments
Contribution Margin = Net Revenue − Direct Variable Costs
Contribution Margin % = Contribution Margin ÷ Net Revenue × 100
Cost per Lead = Attributable Acquisition Spend ÷ Eligible Leads
Customer Acquisition Cost = Attributable Acquisition Cost ÷ Acquired Customers
Average Revenue per Paying Customer = Relevant Revenue ÷ Paying Customers
All calculations must use documented definitions and matching time periods.
If the denominator is zero or the data is incomplete, report the metric as not available rather than assigning a misleading
zero value.
2.75.4 Financial Interpretation Rules
1. Separate realized results from forecasts.
2. Distinguish revenue from gross transaction value.
3. Distinguish paid leads from acquired customers.
4. Disclose unallocated or estimated costs.
5. Avoid comparing incomplete early CAC with assumed lifetime value as if both were validated.
6. Use scenarios for forward-looking economics.
7. Obtain Finance review before executive presentation.

2.79 PHASE 06 — RISK REGISTER & CONTROL ACTIONS
Risk 1 — Critical Incidents Continue After Launch
Impact: Customer experience, trust and service continuity deteriorate.

-- 208 of 211 --

Control: Maintain active incident command, restrict growth exposure and prioritize recovery.
Risk 2 — Early Growth Metrics Are Misinterpreted
Impact: Investment decisions are based on insufficient evidence.
Control: Report sample sizes, measurement periods, data quality and confidence limitations.
Risk 3 — Campaign Spend Increases Before Product Stabilization
Impact: Acquisition cost rises while customers encounter broken journeys.
Control: Require Product and Technology stability clearance before significant campaign expansion.
Risk 4 — Customer Support Demand Exceeds Capacity
Impact: Delayed responses, unresolved cases and customer dissatisfaction.
Control: Monitor backlog and response performance; adjust coverage and acquisition exposure.
Risk 5 — Production Fixes Introduce New Defects
Impact: Service stability deteriorates.
Control: Enforce controlled changes, targeted testing, regression checks and rollback readiness.
Risk 6 — Financial Performance Is Inaccurately Reported
Impact: Leadership makes unsupported investment decisions.
Control: Reconcile revenue, spend and cost definitions with Finance.
Risk 7 — Departmental Ownership Ends After Day 30
Impact: Incidents, support cases and improvement work lose accountability.
Control: Require formal ownership transfer and accepted handoff records.
Risk 8 — Product-Market Fit Is Declared Prematurely
Impact: The organization scales without adequate retention or demand evidence.
Control: Treat the first five days as an early validation window and continue longitudinal measurement.
Risk 9 — Next-Cycle Scope Exceeds Available Capacity
Impact: Quality, delivery and operating performance decline.
Control: Require effort estimation, resource approval and explicit prioritization.
